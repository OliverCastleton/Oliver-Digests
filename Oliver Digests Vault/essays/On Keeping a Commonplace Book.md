For a few centuries, serious readers kept a commonplace book: a notebook of passages copied from whatever they were reading, gathered under headings so they could be found again. John Locke even published a method for indexing one. The habit faded once books became cheap and searchable, which is a shame, because the point was never storage.

The point was the copying. To copy a sentence you have to decide it is worth keeping, and to decide that you have to have understood it. A highlighter asks nothing of you; a pen asks you to slow down. The passages I have written out are the ones I still remember, and the ones I only underlined I mostly do not.

The most famous commonplace book was never meant to be read. The [[Meditations]] of Marcus Aurelius are notes a Roman emperor wrote to himself, in Greek, mostly while on campaign. They are repetitive in the way a real notebook is repetitive: the same few ideas, returned to again and again, because he needed reminding. That is what makes them useful. They show someone practising a way of thinking, not presenting one.

A library of notes, like the one on this site, is a modern version of the same thing. The notes are not reviews. They are a record of what stuck, written soon enough after reading that I can still tell what I thought at the time, and kept so that later I can see how that changed.

Mortimer Adler argued in [[How to Read a Book]] that reading well is active work: you question the book as you go and, in a sense, write a reply. A commonplace book is where that reply ends up.
